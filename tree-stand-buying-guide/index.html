How to choose tree stands

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

To choose the right deer hunting tree stand, start by matching the stand type, hang-on, ladder, climbing, or box, to your hunting style, physical ability, and the terrain you hunt most. Prioritize safety certifications, a weight rating well above your gear-inclusive body weight, and quiet, durable construction that won’t spook deer. Avoid picking a stand solely on price; a few hundred dollars spent on a proven, comfortable, and secure stand repays itself in safer, longer sits.

What are the main types of tree stands and how do they compare?

There are four common categories: hang-on stands, ladder stands, climbing stands, and box blinds. Hang-on stands are lightweight platforms that you attach to the tree with straps or chains; they require separate climbing sticks or screw-in steps to reach hunting height. Ladder stands come as a single unit with a ladder attached to the platform, offering a stable, walk-up entry that is easier for older hunters or anyone wary of climbing. Climbing stands use a two-piece design that you “walk” up the tree by shifting your weight, letting you reach height without extra gear, but they only work on straight, branch-free trees. Box blinds are enclosed, freestanding units that provide full weather protection but are heavy and hard to move. Most mobile hunters prefer a lightweight hang-on from brands like Lone Wolf or Hawk, while landowners often settle on a ladder stand from Millennium or Rivers Edge for comfort and ease.

What safety features should every tree stand have?

At a minimum, any tree stand you consider should meet or exceed the voluntary ASTM F2122 standard, which tests weight capacity and structural integrity. Look for a full-body fall-arrest harness (not just a single strap) with a lineman’s belt and a tether that stays attached from the moment you leave the ground. Many newer stands from Muddy and Summit include the harness in the box, but never assume, check before you buy. Beyond the harness, examine the platform for non-slip surfaces and check that all attachment cables or straps are coated to prevent tree damage and silent movement. Avoid stands that use bare chains against the bark unless you plan to add rubber sheathing. A stable, wobble-free fit is non-negotiable: if a stand shifts when you shift your weight, it’s a fall risk in cold or windy conditions.

Hang-on vs. ladder stand: which is better for your hunting setup?

Hang-on stands are lighter, more packable, and better for public land or remote spots where you need to hike in quietly. They let you adjust height in increments and place the platform exactly where cover demands, but they require practice to set up securely in the dark. Most hang-ons from brands like Lone Wolf or X-Stand weigh under 15 to 20 pounds, making them realistic for a mile-long walk. Ladder stands trade portability for simplicity and comfort. A tall ladder stand (say 15 to 20 feet) gives you a spacious platform and a built-in ladder that makes entry feel as safe as climbing attic stairs. They are ideal for private land setups, young hunters, or anyone with limited mobility. The trade-off is weight, many ladder stands exceed 60 to 80 pounds, and bulk, meaning you likely need an ATV, UTV, or a patient buddy to move them.

How do weight capacity and platform size affect your choice?

Always choose a stand rated for at least 100 to 150 pounds more than your total weight with gear, boots, coat, and backpack. This safety margin accounts for movement, wind, and the extra load of a hanging pack or rifle. Most standard stands from Big Game and Muddy start around a 250 to 300 pound rating, while heavy-duty models go well above 350 pounds. Platform size directly matters for comfort during long sits. A narrow 18-by-24-inch platform works for quick morning hunts but feels cramped after three hours. Larger platforms, around 20 by 30 inches or more, let you shift position, stretch a leg, or turn to shoot without feeling like you’re on a tightrope. Mesh platforms are quieter than solid ones because snow and rain fall through without noise, but solid platforms are warmer in deep cold.

What should you consider about height, tree selection, and setup?

Most deer hunters set stands between 15 and 25 feet high, which keeps your scent above a deer’s normal wind-picking zone and gives a good shooting angle. Avoid the common mistake of going too high: a 30-foot perch can limit shot angles and make recovery of a downed deer harder than you expect. Check local regulations, as some public-land areas restrict maximum stand height. For tree selection, choose a live, straight hardwood or softwood at least 10 to 12 inches in diameter. Avoid dead or leaning trees, pines with brittle bark, or trees with heavy vines that can mask a weak trunk. Before climbing, clear all loose bark and test a few quick tugs on your attachment point. If using climbing sticks, place them at even intervals and secure each one with a strap that doesn’t allow shifting.

How does portability factor into your buying decision?

If you hunt multiple properties or public land, portability is probably your top priority. Look for a hang-on stand that folds flat and weighs under 15 to 18 pounds, and pair it with lightweight climbing sticks that stack or nest. Brands like Lone Wolf and Hawk lead in this category, offering backpack-friendly designs with minimal noise-producing metal-on-metal contact. For hunters who set a single ambush point all season, portability matters far less than comfort and stability. Ladder stands from Millennium and Rivers Edge with padded seats and extra-large platforms are worth carrying in once, even if they require a truck or ATV to move. Remember that any stand you dread hauling is a stand you’ll leave in the garage, so be honest about your willingness to carry gear a mile before dawn.

Frequently asked questions

Are tree stands safe for beginners?

Yes, but only if you buy a stand with a full-body harness, practice ground-level setup first, and never climb without staying attached to the tree. Ladder stands are generally easier and safer for first-time users because they eliminate the need for climbing sticks or steps. Take a hunter-safety course that includes treestand instruction and always tell someone where you’re hunting and when you’ll return.

How high should I set up my tree stand for deer hunting?

Most hunters find 15 to 20 feet strikes the right balance between scent control and shot angle. At this height, your scent disperses above a deer’s nose in most wind conditions, and the shooting lanes stay manageable. Going higher than 25 feet can create steep downward shots that limit your target zone and make it harder to place an ethical hit.

What’s the best tree stand for someone with limited mobility?

A ladder stand with wide, gently angled steps and a seat at a comfortable climbing height is the best choice. Brands like Millennium make ladder stands with specially designed climbing rungs that reduce the strain on knees and hips. Avoid hang-on stands and climbing stands, which require more upper-body strength and balance.

Can I hunt from a hang-on stand without climbing sticks?

Technically you could use screw-in steps or a climbing system, but climbing sticks are safer, faster, and cause less damage to the tree. Screw-in steps are banned on many public lands and weaken the tree, and they’re harder to install in the dark or cold. For a hang-on setup, buy a stack of at least four lightweight climbing sticks from the same manufacturer if possible.

How often should I replace my tree stand?

Inspect any stand annually for rust, cracked welds, frayed straps, or broken cables, and replace the stand immediately if you find damage. Straps and harnesses degrade after three to five years, especially if left exposed to sun and rain. Many manufacturers recommend retiring a stand after five to seven years of regular use, even if it looks fine, because metal fatigue is invisible.

Do I still need a safety harness with a ladder stand?

Yes, absolutely. A ladder stand feels more stable than a hang-on, but falls from ladder stands are just as deadly, often because hunters get complacent once they reach the platform. Wear your full-body harness from the moment your feet leave the ground and stay tethered until you’re back on the last rung. A harness is the single cheapest insurance you can buy.

In shortChoosing a tree stand is ultimately a decision about where you hunt, how you hunt, and what kind of physical effort you’re willing to invest for a successful season. A safe, comfortable stand that fits your style and your woods will keep you in the tree longer, and time in the tree is the only thing that consistently brings deer into range. Buy quality, inspect your gear every year, and never climb without a harness. That’s the short path to a long hunting life.
